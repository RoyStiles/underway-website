Boat Documents: What to Keep Aboard, and When It Expires

Every boat collects paperwork: registration, insurance, licenses, radio and beacon registrations, marina contracts. Most of it sits in a drawer until the day someone asks for it: a marine patrol officer at the ramp, the marina office, an insurer after a claim, or a buyer when you sell.

This guide covers which papers matter, which ones must be aboard, which ones expire, and how to keep them where you can find them. Rules differ by state and country, so check your own state’s boating agency for the exact requirements.

Step 1: Registration or documentation

In the US, most boats with an engine are either state-registered or federally documented.

  • State registration: the certificate of number must be aboard whenever the boat is in use, with the numbers and current decal displayed on the hull as your state requires. Renewal is every one to three years, depending on the state.
  • Coast Guard documentation: available for boats of 5 net tons or more. The original Certificate of Documentation must be aboard, and it must be renewed on the term you chose. A documented boat may also need a state registration or decal, depending on the state.
  • Title: many states title boats, and some title outboard motors and trailers too. Keep titles at home, not aboard.

Step 2: Insurance

Keep a copy of the declarations page aboard. It is the page marinas, yards and harbormasters ask for. Know what is on it:

  • The policy dates, and the insurer’s claims phone number
  • Navigation limits: the waters you are covered in
  • Any lay-up period, when the boat is meant to be out of the water or not in use
  • Named operators, if your policy lists them
  • Survey requirements: insurers often ask for a new survey on older boats every few years

Many marinas require proof of liability insurance and ask to be named on the certificate. Send it before the slip contract renews.

Step 3: Licenses and education cards

  • Boater education card: many states require one for some or all operators, often based on age. Carry it when you run the boat.
  • Captain’s license: needed if you carry paying passengers. It has an expiration date and medical and drug-testing requirements to keep current.

Step 4: Radio and beacon registrations

  • MMSI number: the number that makes your VHF radio’s DSC distress button identify your boat. Keep it with your boat details, and keep the registration current when you change boats or contact details.
  • Ship station and operator licenses: in the US, a recreational boat using VHF in domestic waters usually needs no FCC license. Traveling to foreign ports, or using HF/SSB radio, does.
  • EPIRB and PLB registration: register every beacon with NOAA, and renew it every two years. Note the beacon’s battery and hydrostatic release dates as well.

Step 5: Marina, travel and ownership papers

  • Slip or storage contract: the term, renewal date and what the marina needs from you each year
  • Customs decal: in the US, boats 30 feet and over need a CBP user fee decal to enter from abroad, renewed every calendar year
  • Cruising permits for other countries, with their dates
  • Bill of sale, survey and lien release from when you bought the boat; a buyer will ask for them when you sell
  • Inspection records: a Vessel Safety Check decal, fire suppression and life raft service, with their dates

Step 6: Originals aboard, copies everywhere

Keep the papers that must be aboard in one waterproof folder, in the same place every time. Keep titles and anything not required aboard at home. Then:

  • Photograph or scan every page, front and back, so you have copies on your phone that work with no signal
  • Write down every expiration date in one list, with the date you need to act (a month or two before)
  • Replace the copies when a renewal comes in, so you never show an expired one

Most of these papers hold personal details. Keep the copies somewhere private, not in a shared album.
