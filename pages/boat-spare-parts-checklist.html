Keeping the Right Spare Parts on Board

Most breakdowns on the water come from small, cheap parts: a fuel filter, a belt, an impeller, a fuse. The part costs a few dollars. The tow costs hundreds. A small, well-chosen kit aboard turns many of these from a ruined day into a ten-minute fix.

The goal is not to carry a parts store. It is to carry the right few items, know where they are, and replace them when you use them.

Step 1: Decide what is worth carrying

Ask two questions about each part:

  1. Is it likely to fail or wear out? Impellers, belts, filters, fuses and bulbs are the usual suspects.
  2. Would a failure stop the boat or make it unsafe, and could you fit the part yourself on the water? If yes to both, carry it.

Skip parts you cannot fit without a shop, unless you are cruising somewhere remote.

Step 2: Match the kit to your engine

Always check your manual for the exact part numbers. These are the usual items.

Outboards

  • Spare propeller, with the nut, washer and cotter pin or locking tab
  • Spark plugs, and the plug wrench
  • Fuel filter or water-separator element
  • Spare shear pin or hardware if your prop uses one
  • Spare engine oil and a quart of lower unit oil

Sterndrives and inboard gas engines

  • Water pump impeller, with gasket or cover seal
  • Drive belt or belts
  • Fuel filter and water-separator element
  • Spare engine oil, plus power-steering or trim fluid as your engine needs
  • Distributor cap and rotor or ignition parts if your engine uses them

Inboard diesels

  • Primary and secondary fuel filters
  • Oil filter and oil
  • Water pump impeller
  • Belts, and spare hose clamps and a length of hose
  • Coolant

Step 3: Add the general boat kit

These help on any boat:

  • Fuses and spare bulbs for your electrical panel and nav lights
  • Hose clamps in several sizes
  • Spare line, zip ties and electrical tape
  • A basic tool kit that fits your fasteners
  • Spare bilge pump or float switch if your boat relies on one
  • Spare battery terminals, and a jump pack or a second battery if you cruise far
  • A spare key, and a spare plug or lanyard for kill switches

Step 4: Store it so you can find it

A part you cannot find is a part you do not have. Store spares:

  • Together, in one labeled box or bag
  • Dry, in sealed bags, with rubber and filters away from oil and sun
  • With the tools needed to fit them
  • With a printed list of what is in the box

Step 5: Count, date and replace

Parts age and get used. Keep a simple list with:

  • Part name and your engine it fits
  • Brand and part number
  • How many you have, and the minimum you want aboard
  • Where it is stored
  • When it was bought, for items that age, such as belts and rubber

When you use a part, replace it that week and note the new count. Do not wait for the next season.

Step 6: Link parts to the jobs that use them

A job like “change the impeller” always needs the same parts. If you know that ahead, you can check you have everything before you start, and you will not find out halfway through that the gasket is missing.
