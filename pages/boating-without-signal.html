Boating Without Cell Signal: What to Have Ready

Cell coverage on the water is patchy. It fades a few miles offshore, drops out behind headlands and in remote anchorages, and disappears on many rivers and lakes away from towns. A phone that works fine at the slip may show no bars when you need it most.

None of that is a problem if you plan for it. This guide covers what to set up before you leave, so the boat, the crew and the information you need all work with no signal.

Step 1: Make the VHF your first call

A VHF radio is the one piece of gear made for this. It reaches the Coast Guard, towing services, marinas, bridges and other boats, with no cell network at all.

  • Channel 16 is for distress and hailing. Know how to make a Mayday call before you need to.
  • A DSC radio has a red distress button that sends your boat’s identity and position. It only works if your MMSI number is programmed in and the radio is connected to a GPS.
  • Carry a handheld VHF too, charged, in case the boat’s power or antenna fails.
  • The weather channels (WX) carry NOAA forecasts and warnings in most US coastal and Great Lakes waters.

Step 2: Download charts and weather before you leave

  • Charts: carry paper charts for your area, or make sure your chartplotter has current charts loaded. If you use a phone or tablet app, download the charts for the whole trip while you have a connection.
  • Weather: check the marine forecast and any warnings before you go, and save or screenshot it. Weather maps in apps don’t refresh offshore.
  • Destinations: note the VHF channel, phone number and approach notes for each marina or anchorage you plan to use, and a fallback if the weather turns.

Step 3: File a float plan while you still have signal

Send your float plan from the dock, not from the water. Tell the person holding it that you may be out of range, and when they should start to worry. If your plans change, tell them by VHF relay, at your next stop with signal, or when you’re back. See How to file a float plan.

Step 4: Carry a way to call for help by satellite

  • EPIRB or PLB: sends a distress signal with your position by satellite, anywhere. Register it with NOAA and keep the registration current.
  • Satellite messenger: lets you send short messages or check-ins with no cell signal, so the person holding your float plan knows you’re fine.
  • Some newer phones can send emergency messages by satellite. Learn how before you rely on it, and don’t make it your only plan.

Step 5: Keep your boat’s information on board

When something goes wrong far from shore, the answers are usually in a manual or a parts list. Make sure you can open them without a connection:

  • Engine and equipment manuals saved as files on your phone or tablet, not just as web links
  • The part numbers of the filters, impellers and belts you carry, and where they are stored
  • Copies of your registration or documentation and insurance (see Boat documents)
  • Your towing service membership number and their VHF channel and phone number
  • Your MMSI, HIN and a description of the boat, ready to read out on the radio

Step 6: Power and backups

A phone hunting for a signal drains its battery fast. Turn on airplane mode, or low power mode, when you’re out of range and don’t need it. Carry a charging cable for the boat and a charged power bank. Keep paper copies of the few things you can’t do without: a chart, the emergency numbers, and the Mayday script.

Step 7: Catch up when you’re back in range

Write down engine hours, fuel added and anything that needs fixing while you’re on the water, then bring your records up to date when you’re back. Close your float plan with the person holding it as soon as you have signal.

This guide is general information. It is not a substitute for proper training, a working VHF radio and the safety equipment your boat and waters require. In an emergency, call the Coast Guard on VHF channel 16.
