## How to Set Up a Boat Engine Maintenance Schedule

A boat engine rarely fails without warning. It usually fails because an oil change, an impeller or a fuel filter was put off until "next time." A simple schedule, written down once and kept up, is the cheapest insurance you can buy for a day on the water.

This guide shows how to build one for any engine, and what to put in it.

### Step 1: Start with your engine manual

Your owner's manual or service manual is the only source for your exact intervals. The numbers in this guide are typical, not a replacement. Write down, for each engine:

- Make, model and year
- Serial number
- Fuel type (gasoline or diesel)
- Where it lives (port, starboard, outboard, generator)

If you bought the boat used and the manual is missing, the maker's website usually has a free copy.

### Step 2: Track by engine hours, then add the calendar

Most service intervals are set in **engine hours**, not months. A boat that runs 40 hours a year and one that runs 300 hours a year need different schedules.

Use both clocks and service on whichever comes first:

- **Hours:** oil and filter, lower unit oil, belts, spark plugs, fuel filters
- **Calendar:** winterizing, impeller checks, anodes, hoses and clamps, battery checks

A common rule is "every 100 hours or once a year, whichever comes first." Low-hour boats are the reason the calendar half matters: oil and rubber parts age even when the engine sits.

### Step 3: List the jobs for your engine type

These are the usual items. Confirm each against your manual.

**Outboards**
- Engine oil and filter (four-stroke)
- Lower unit gear oil
- Water pump impeller
- Fuel filter and water separator
- Spark plugs
- Anodes (zincs)
- Grease points, steering and controls

**Sterndrives**
- Engine oil and filter
- Drive (outdrive) oil
- Impeller
- Gimbal bearing and bellows (inspect for cracks and leaks)
- Belts and coolant
- Fuel filter and water separator
- Anodes and propeller

**Inboard diesels**
- Engine oil and filters
- Fuel filters and water separator (primary and secondary)
- Impeller and raw-water strainer
- Belts, coolant and heat exchanger
- Air filter
- Shaft seal, stuffing box and anodes

**Generators**
- Oil and filter, fuel filter, impeller, belts, and a regular run under load

### Step 4: Add the seasonal jobs

Hours do not catch everything. Put these on the calendar:

- **Spring launch:** fluids, hoses and clamps, belts, battery, fresh fuel, test run
- **Mid-season:** check the water separator and strainer, top off fluids
- **Haul-out or winterizing:** fuel stabilizer, fogging or storage per your manual, change oil, drain or protect cooling water where freezing is possible

### Step 5: Write it down every time

A schedule only helps if you record what was done. For every service, log:

- Date and engine hours
- What was done
- Parts used (brand and part number)
- Who did it, you or a shop
- Receipts and photos

A clean service history also helps when you sell. Buyers and surveyors trust a boat that can show its records.

### Step 6: Watch for the next due date

Every task should have a next-due number: the hours or the date at which it comes up again. Check it before every trip, not just at the start of the season.
